# Album artwork

The creative brief the album cover is generated from. The album's name, the
artist's name and the combined technical style are supplied above this brief;
the producer's own description of the cover, where they gave one, is supplied
below it.

## What a cover has to do

It is seen at 300 pixels square in a library and at 40 pixels in a now-playing
bar. Everything follows from that:

- **One subject.** A cover with three things in it has none at that size.
- **High contrast, few colours.** Three or four, one of them dominant.
- **Composition centred or strongly off-centre.** Anything in between turns to
  mush when it is scaled down.
- **No small detail.** Texture yes, detail no.

## Text

Do not render the artist name or the album title into the image unless the
producer explicitly asked for it. Image models set type badly — misspelt,
misaligned, in a font nobody chose — and a cover that is wrong in a way that
cannot be fixed is worse than a cover with no words on it. Where they did ask,
keep it to the album name alone, large, and say so plainly in the prompt.

## Matching the record

Read the technical style, not just the genre. A record described as "tape
saturation, room mics, brushed snare, deliberate headroom" wants a photographic
cover with grain in it; one described as "FM bass, gated drums, wide stereo pads"
wants something graphic and synthetic. Let the production character decide the
medium.

Where the producer described the cover themselves, that description leads and
this brief only fills the gaps.

## Format

Square, 1024×1024. Full bleed — no border, no frame, no mockup of a record
sleeve on a table. The image *is* the cover, not a photograph of one.

## Avoid

- Vinyl records, cassettes, turntables and headphones as subjects. They say
  "music" and nothing else.
- Collage of unrelated objects.
- Anything that reads as stock photography.
- Watermarks, signatures and logos.
- Faces, unless the producer asked for one — an invented face on a record cover
  is a person who does not exist being presented as the artist.

## Revisions

When the producer asks for a change, revise the brief that made the current
image rather than writing a new one from scratch. "Make it bluer" means the same
cover in a different palette, not a different cover.
